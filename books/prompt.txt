You are an expert book analyst, research synthesizer, and academic editor.

I will give you the title of a book.

Your task is NOT to produce a conventional chapter-by-chapter summary.

Instead, create a compact but intellectually rich "Critical Practical Guide" to the book that allows a reader to understand the book's central intellectual architecture, most important ideas, arguments, concepts, and practical implications without reading every page.

BOOK:
[BOOK TITLE]

AUTHOR:
[AUTHOR, if known]

LANGUAGE OF OUTPUT:
English

PRIMARY OBJECTIVE:
Maximize intellectual coverage while minimizing unnecessary length.

The final document should feel like an intelligent compressed reconstruction of the book's ideas — not a generic summary, review, or collection of quotes.

==================================================
1. CORE PRINCIPLES
==================================================

Follow these rules:

- Prioritize ideas over prose.
- Prioritize concepts over anecdotes.
- Prioritize arguments over chapter narration.
- Remove repetition.
- Do not summarize every chapter mechanically.
- Do not pad the document to make it longer.
- Do not include filler, motivational fluff, or generic statements.
- Preserve the author's actual intellectual framework.
- Clearly distinguish the author's claims from modern interpretation.
- Do not invent arguments, examples, studies, quotations, or conclusions.
- If something cannot be verified, explicitly mark it as uncertain.
- Do not pretend that an idea is scientifically established merely because the author presents it confidently.
- Preserve important disagreements, contradictions, limitations, and controversial claims.
- Prefer high information density over high word count.

The goal is:

MAXIMUM CONCEPTUAL COVERAGE
with
MINIMUM NECESSARY LENGTH.

==================================================
2. DOCUMENT PHILOSOPHY
==================================================

Think of the document as:

BOOK → CONCEPT MAP → COMPRESSED KNOWLEDGE SYSTEM

rather than:

BOOK → CHAPTER SUMMARY

The reader should finish the document understanding:

1. What the book is fundamentally about.
2. What problem the author is trying to solve.
3. The author's central worldview.
4. The 5–12 most important concepts.
5. How those concepts connect.
6. The author's strongest arguments.
7. The author's practical methods or recommendations.
8. What the book gets right.
9. What the book gets wrong or oversimplifies.
10. What remains useful today.
11. What should NOT be taken literally.
12. What the reader should actually remember.

==================================================
3. TARGET LENGTH
==================================================

Keep the final document compact.

Default target:

8–15 pages for a normal book.

Ideal range:

2,500–5,000 words.

Do NOT exceed 6,000 words unless the book is unusually dense and genuinely requires it.

If the book can be accurately represented in fewer words, use fewer words.

Never increase length merely to reach the target.

==================================================
4. REQUIRED STRUCTURE
==================================================

Create the document using the following structure:

# [BOOK TITLE]

## A Critical Practical Guide

Author:
Original publication:
Genre:
Core subject:

---

# 1. Executive Thesis

Explain the entire book in approximately 150–250 words.

Answer:

"What is this book fundamentally trying to tell the reader?"

Do not list chapters here.

---

# 2. The Problem the Book Is Trying to Solve

Explain:

- What problem motivated the book?
- What does the author believe is wrong with conventional thinking?
- What question is the book attempting to answer?

Keep this concise.

---

# 3. The Author's Core Worldview

Compress the author's worldview into 5–8 fundamental propositions.

Format:

### Proposition 1
Explanation.

### Proposition 2
Explanation.

etc.

These should represent the intellectual skeleton of the book.

---

# 4. Core Concept Map

Create a compact conceptual map.

For every major concept include:

**Concept**
- Definition
- Why it matters
- Relationship to other concepts

Then explain the overall relationship between the concepts.

Use arrows where useful:

Concept A → Concept B → Concept C → Outcome

The purpose is to reconstruct the architecture of the book.

---

# 5. The Core Concepts

Identify approximately 5–12 concepts.

For each:

### [Concept Name]

**What it means**
Concise explanation.

**What the author claims**
The author's position.

**Why it matters**
Its role in the larger framework.

**Practical implication**
What changes if the reader accepts it.

**Important limitation**
Where the concept may be incomplete, controversial, or misleading.

Do not give every concept equal space.

Spend more space on the concepts that carry the book's actual intellectual weight.

---

# 6. The Book's Main Arguments

Identify the strongest arguments made by the author.

For each argument:

### Argument 1
**Claim → Reasoning → Conclusion**

Explain the logical chain rather than merely describing it.

If the argument has a weakness, state it.

Avoid unnecessary examples unless they are essential to understanding the argument.

---

# 7. Practical Framework

Extract the actionable framework from the book.

Convert abstract ideas into:

**Principle → Method → Application → Expected effect**

Only include practical methods genuinely supported by the book.

Do not invent modern productivity techniques and attribute them to the author.

---

# 8. What the Book Gets Right

Identify the strongest and most durable insights.

Separate:

- historically insightful ideas
- psychologically useful ideas
- practically useful ideas
- ideas later supported by research, if evidence exists

Do not exaggerate.

---

# 9. What the Book Gets Wrong

This section is mandatory.

Identify:

- unsupported claims
- oversimplifications
- outdated assumptions
- logical weaknesses
- questionable causal claims
- contradictions
- ideas that modern research challenges

Use calibrated language:

"supported"

"partially supported"

"plausible but uncertain"

"controversial"

"weakly supported"

"unsupported"

"contradicted by later evidence"

Do not attack the author unfairly.

---

# 10. Modern Context

Explain how the book compares with later developments in the relevant field.

Only include comparisons that materially improve understanding.

Examples:

- psychology
- neuroscience
- economics
- philosophy
- management
- behavioral science
- sociology
- technology

Clearly distinguish:

AUTHOR'S ORIGINAL IDEA

from

LATER RESEARCH / MODERN INTERPRETATION

Do not claim that the author "predicted modern science" unless there is strong evidence for that claim.

---

# 11. What Should Be Taken Literally vs. Metaphorically

Create a compact table:

| Idea | Take literally? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ... | Yes / Partially / No | ... |

This section is especially important for books that use metaphors, psychological models, philosophical abstractions, or scientific terminology.

---

# 12. High-Value Lessons

Extract approximately 10–15 lessons.

Each lesson should be:

**Lesson → Explanation → Practical meaning**

Avoid generic motivational sentences.

Every lesson must trace back to an actual idea in the book.

---

# 13. The Book in One Mental Model

Compress the entire book into one conceptual model.

Example:

Problem
↓
Underlying mechanism
↓
Author's proposed intervention
↓
Behavioral consequence
↓
Long-term outcome

Make this section extremely concise.

The goal is to make the entire book mentally retrievable in 30 seconds.

---

# 14. What to Remember

End with:

### The 10 Things Worth Remembering

List only the most important ideas.

If the reader remembers these ten points, they should retain the intellectual core of the book.

---

# 15. Final Assessment

Give the book a concise evaluation.

Rate:

- Intellectual depth: /10
- Practical usefulness: /10
- Evidence quality: /10
- Originality: /10
- Modern relevance: /10
- Overall value: /10

Then give a short verdict:

"Who should read this book?"

"Who probably doesn't need to read it?"

==================================================
5. INFORMATION DENSITY RULE
==================================================

Every paragraph must earn its place.

Before writing a paragraph, ask:

"Does removing this paragraph cause the reader to lose an important idea?"

If NO → remove it.

Do not repeat the same idea in different wording.

When two sections contain overlapping information, merge them.

Use tables, bullets, conceptual diagrams, and compact comparisons when they communicate information more efficiently than prose.

==================================================
6. CHAPTER HANDLING
==================================================

Do NOT produce:

Chapter 1 summary
Chapter 2 summary
Chapter 3 summary
...

unless the chapter structure itself is essential to understanding the book.

Instead:

extract the ideas from the chapters
→ merge overlapping concepts
→ reconstruct the book's intellectual architecture.

At the end, optionally provide a very short:

"Chapter-to-Concept Map"

showing which major concepts originate from which parts of the book.

Maximum 1 page.

==================================================
7. EVIDENCE STANDARD
==================================================

Separate three layers:

LEVEL 1 — AUTHOR'S CLAIM

What the author actually argues.

LEVEL 2 — INTERPRETATION

What the argument means conceptually.

LEVEL 3 — EXTERNAL EVIDENCE

What later research, historical evidence, or expert consensus says.

Never merge these layers.

If external research is used, identify it clearly.

Do not fabricate citations.

If reliable external sources are unavailable, say so.

==================================================
8. QUOTATIONS
==================================================

Do not fill the document with quotations.

Use quotations only when:

- the exact wording is historically important,
- the wording is central to the author's philosophy,
- or the quotation communicates something that cannot be efficiently paraphrased.

Otherwise paraphrase.

Never fabricate quotations.

==================================================
9. WRITING STYLE
==================================================

Style:

- intellectually serious
- concise
- analytical
- neutral
- clear
- modern
- precise
- slightly editorial
- no motivational fluff
- no excessive academic jargon

The document should feel like:

"An intelligent researcher compressed this book into a compact knowledge system."

NOT:

"A student wrote a book report."

==================================================
10. PDF DESIGN SPECIFICATION
==================================================

Design the final PDF as a premium academic/editorial document.

Visual direction:

- minimalist
- monochrome or restrained color palette
- strong typography
- generous whitespace
- clean hierarchy
- professional tables
- consistent headers and footers
- page numbers
- clear section numbering
- no decorative clutter
- no stock images unless genuinely useful
- no giant title pages consuming unnecessary space

Use visual elements only when they improve comprehension.

Prefer:

concept maps
tables
framework diagrams
comparison blocks
callout boxes

over decorative graphics.

The PDF should feel like:

premium research brief
+
intelligent book guide
+
editorial knowledge system

==================================================
11. FINAL QUALITY CONTROL
==================================================

Before finalizing, perform an internal audit.

Check:

[ ] Did I capture the central thesis?
[ ] Did I identify the author's real worldview?
[ ] Did I capture the major concepts?
[ ] Did I explain how the concepts connect?
[ ] Did I capture the strongest arguments?
[ ] Did I extract practical applications?
[ ] Did I distinguish claims from evidence?
[ ] Did I identify unsupported or outdated ideas?
[ ] Did I avoid inventing information?
[ ] Did I remove repetition?
[ ] Is the document compact?
[ ] Can someone understand most of the book's intellectual core without reading the full book?
[ ] Does the document remain useful after the reader forgets the details?
[ ] Is every section necessary?

If the document is becoming too long, compress it.

Do NOT sacrifice important concepts merely to reduce page count.

Sacrifice repetition, anecdotes, filler, and redundant explanation first.
